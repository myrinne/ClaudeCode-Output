## Title (108 characters, unchanged; decision open)
**AI Decision Support in Periodic Occupational Health Examinations: A Systematized Review of Evidence and Gaps**

Alternative: **Artificial Intelligence for Interpreting Occupational Health Examination Tests: A Systematized Review of Evidence and Gaps** (124 characters)

## Abstract (253 words including the five subheadings; limit 255)

**Introduction** Periodic occupational health examinations generate large volumes of test results. Whether artificial intelligence (AI) can validly interpret these results remains unclear.

**Methods** This systematized review followed PRISMA 2020 reporting. PubMed, Scopus, ProQuest and IEEE Xplore were searched 5 October 2026 for studies (2006 onwards) in which AI interpreted occupational examination tests of workers or ex-workers against a clinical reference standard. Models forecasting future disease were excluded. Quality was appraised with the JBI diagnostic test accuracy checklist; studies with case-control design, ≥2 unmet patient-selection items, or incomplete analysis were rated low quality.

**Results** Of 861 records screened, 13 studies were included: nine on chest radiography, one each on audiometry and electrocardiography, and two on multimodal records. No spirometry, laboratory or Southeast Asian study was included. Imaging area under the curve (AUC) ranged 0.84–1.00 (eight studies); sensitivity (62–100%) and specificity (46–98%; seven studies each) varied with threshold. Both multimodal studies used physicians' fit/unfit status as reference (AUC 0.60; kappa 1.00 against one physician rating documents). Two studies enrolled consecutive samples, three performed external validation, two reported negative predictive value, none evaluated AI prospectively, and eight were rated low quality.

**Discussion** Evidence centres on chest radiography, mostly from datasets likely overstating screening performance. Clinical-setting spirometry and ECG AI remains unvalidated in workers. No study assessed fitness to work against job demands.

**Conclusion** Evidence is insufficient to support AI interpretation in periodic examinations. AI should remain decision support under physician responsibility. Adoption requires external validation, negative predictive value reporting, comparison with occupational physicians, and prospective evaluation.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence; chest radiography; clinical decision support; occupational health surveillance; pneumoconiosis

## Reply to the editor

Dear Editor,

Thank you for your helpful comments. We have revised the abstract as follows.

1. **Appraisal tool.** We used the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies; QUADAS-2 was not used. "High concern" has been replaced by "low quality", and the Methods now state the criterion: a study was rated low quality if it used a case-control design, did not meet two or more patient-selection items, or did not analyse all participants.
2. **Discussion data.** The findings on physicians' fit/unfit status (AUC 0.60; kappa 1.00 against one physician rating documents) have been moved to the Results. The Discussion now gives interpretation only.
3. **Denominators.** The Results now state that the AUC range comes from eight studies and the sensitivity and specificity ranges from seven studies each.
4. **Study counts.** "n=" has been replaced with words ("nine", "one each", "two").

The revised abstract remains within the 255-word limit.

Kind regards,

Refs 14–19 (Discussion context, from outside the 13 included studies; PubMed PMIDs 30765505, 30617318, 39033067, 37143227, 37129490, 42629289):
- **14, 15 → abstract Discussion sentence** "Clinical-setting spirometry and ECG AI remains unvalidated in workers." AI matched ATS/ERS lung-function patterns in 100% of cases vs 74% for 120 pulmonologists, with correct diagnosis 82% vs 45% (14); AI-ECG detected LV ejection fraction ≤35% with AUC 0.93 in 52,870 patients (15). Both are clinical populations, not workers.
- **16, 17 → poster "So what for Sp.Ok" / paper Discussion.** 12 commercial TB-CAD products differed in accuracy, thresholds varied by product and population, and all did worst in older people, people with previous TB and people with HIV (16); CAD accuracy for non-TB abnormalities is not yet evaluated (17). TB-oriented CAD cannot be assumed to detect pneumoconiosis or to use a fixed threshold in Indonesian MCU.
- **18, 19 → poster / paper, next to the conclusion.** With incorrect AI suggestions, even very experienced radiologists' accuracy fell from 82% to 46% (automation bias) (18); in a prospective crossover study, four commercial chest-radiograph AIs did not improve accuracy and increased false positives, though reading was faster (19). Physician oversight is necessary but not by itself a safeguard.
- Ref 14 page: Eur Respir J article number 1801660 (from the DOI; PubMed lists volume and issue only). Ref 19 is online ahead of print; add volume and pages when issued.
